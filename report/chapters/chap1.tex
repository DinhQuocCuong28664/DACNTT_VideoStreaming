\section{Background and Rationale}
\label{sec:background}

Video has become the dominant form of content on the Internet, and platforms such as YouTube and
Vimeo have made uploading and sharing video an everyday activity. Behind that apparent simplicity
lies a demanding engineering problem. A source file may use any container, codec, resolution or
frame rate, yet the platform must deliver a stream that starts quickly on every device and adapts
to whatever bandwidth the viewer has at that moment.

The industry answer is HTTP Live Streaming (HLS)~\cite{rfc8216}. HLS cuts a video into short
segments and encodes the same content at several quality levels, called renditions. A master
playlist lists the renditions, each rendition has its own media playlist of segments, and the
player chooses among them while it plays according to the throughput it measures. This mechanism,
Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR), lets playback lower its quality when a connection weakens instead
of stalling.

Producing those renditions is expensive, and this is where the architectural difficulty lies.
Transcoding is a bursty workload: it needs many processor cores while a video is being encoded and
none while nobody uploads. A platform that keeps servers running to wait for uploads pays for idle
capacity most of the time, while a platform sized for the average load cannot absorb a burst of
uploads and makes them wait. This tension between idle cost and burst capacity motivates the
project. Instead of keeping compute allocated, the system described here starts containers only
when a video arrives and releases them as soon as the work is done, so that processing costs money
only while it runs.

\section{Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project builds a complete video sharing platform together with the automated transcoding
pipeline behind it, and has five objectives. The first is a web platform on which users register,
upload videos, watch them at several quality levels, and take part in a community through
channels, subscriptions, comments, playlists and memberships, with the visibility of each video
chosen by its owner. The second is a fully event-driven pipeline: the arrival of a source file in
Amazon S3 alone starts the processing, which is coordinated by AWS Step Functions and ends with an
encrypted HLS package served through a content delivery network. The third is to run every FFmpeg
job in a container on AWS Batch with AWS Fargate, created on demand and released when the queue is
empty, using Spot capacity first and On-Demand capacity only as overflow. The fourth is to describe
the whole environment as Infrastructure as Code and to check every change automatically with a
continuous integration pipeline that also runs security scans. The fifth is to evaluate the
result on the deployed system: playback start time and quality, transcoding time from a two-minute
clip to a four-hour video, behaviour under one hundred simultaneous uploads, and operating cost.

The first objective defines what the platform offers its users, and the second and third how the
processing is carried out; on-demand containers driven by events are the central hypothesis of the
project. The fourth makes the system reproducible and safe to change, and the fifth turns the
claims of the other four into measurements. Table~\ref{tab:objective-results} returns to each
objective with its result.

\section{Scope and Deliverables}
\label{sec:scope}

The project delivers the full vertical slice of a video sharing service: a single-page web
application with its own HLS player, a REST API, the transcoding workflow and its container image,
the cloud infrastructure that hosts them, and the scripts used to deploy and measure them. Besides
viewers and creators, the platform serves three staff roles, operator, auditor and administrator,
who review flagged content, audit the automatic screening and manage accounts. The production
system runs at the domain \href{https://zelostech.site}{zelostech.site}.

Several areas are deliberately left out. The project does not implement digital rights management
such as Widevine or FairPlay: its AES-128 encryption keeps segments unreadable to anyone without
access, but cannot stop an authorised viewer from saving what they watch. It does not implement live
streaming, since the pipeline is designed for video on demand. It does not optimise the bitrate
ladder per title; every video receives the rungs of a fixed six-rung ladder from 144p to 1080p that
its source resolution can fill, and each rung uses a quality-targeted encoding with a bitrate cap.
Payments for channel memberships run in a sandbox, and the production database runs on the free
tier of MongoDB Atlas, whose limits are measured and discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The work combines a review of existing practice with an iterative build-and-measure cycle.

The theoretical part established how the problem is solved at industrial scale before the design
was fixed. Engineering accounts from Netflix describe how a large platform splits video processing
into independent steps and tunes its encoding per title~\cite{netflix2024microservices,
netflix2015pertitle}, and the AWS Video on Demand solution shows the managed-service equivalent,
in which a workflow engine owns each job and every step reports its state~\cite{awsvod}. The
protocol was taken from its specification~\cite{rfc8216} and the encoding parameters from the
FFmpeg documentation~\cite{ffmpegdocs}, rather than from secondary sources. Studies of serverless
video processing supplied the motivation for the architecture~\cite{grid2023serverless,
nossdav2019video}.

The practical part followed an incremental model. The infrastructure and the transcoding workflow
were built first and run on a staging environment, then the API, then the web application, and
only then was the production environment created from the same Terraform code. Every push and pull
request runs linting, type checks, unit tests, a smoke test of the transcoder image and the security
scans, so each change is reviewed before it is merged.

The evaluation was empirical. Every figure in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} was produced by a
measurement script kept in the repository and run against the production system in its final
configuration, and the raw output of each run was stored as a JSON file;
Table~\ref{tab:provenance} maps each table and figure to the run that produced it.

\section{Practical Significance}
\label{sec:significance}

The direct beneficiaries of this design are small teams and individual creators who need a video
platform but cannot justify a transcoding cluster that runs all the time. Because compute exists
only while a job runs, the cost of the platform follows the number of uploads rather than the
number of hours in a month; Section~\ref{sec:cost-analysis} compares it with an always-on server
sized for the same work. This makes video hosting affordable for a service whose uploads are
intermittent, which describes most platforms before they grow large.

The design also addresses a requirement that is easy to state and easy to get wrong. Marking a video
private means nothing if its segments remain reachable by anyone who knows their address. Here the
processed files stay in a private bucket that only the content delivery network can read, the
network accepts only requests signed for one video, and the segments are encrypted with a key that
the API releases only after checking access. This scheme can be reused by any application that
serves user-owned media from object storage.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The main contribution of this work is an end-to-end demonstration that serverless containers,
coordinated by a workflow engine, can serve as the transcoding backbone of a video platform, with a
measured account of what that choice costs and what it delivers. Several parts can be reused on
their own. An upload event travels from Amazon S3 through a queue to a Step Functions execution
named after the video and its upload version, so a duplicated event never starts a second run.
The FFmpeg graph decodes the source once and produces every rendition with aligned keyframes, so the
player can switch quality at any segment boundary. Long videos are cut into chunks on keyframe
boundaries, encoded in parallel as one Batch array job and stitched into ordinary playlists; a
four-hour source becomes playable in about thirty-eight minutes within the capacity of one AWS
account. Finally, the processing containers hold no database credentials: they report progress
through events, and a single function owns every processing state written to the database.

\section{Structure of the Report}
\label{sec:structure}

The rest of this report is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:literature} reviews comparable
platforms, explains the models behind the design and justifies the choice of technologies.
Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements} describes the users and stakeholders, states the functional and
non-functional requirements, and specifies the use cases. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} presents the
architecture, analyses the transcoding workflow and the HLS output in detail, and describes the
database, the API, the interface, security and content moderation.
Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} covers the implementation of each part, the infrastructure code,
the problems met during development and the automated tests. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}
presents the deployed system and measures playback, transcoding, behaviour under load and cost, then
sets the requirements against this evidence. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} compares the results with
the objectives and states the strengths, limitations and future work.
