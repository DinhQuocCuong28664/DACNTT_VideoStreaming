\section{Background and Rationale}
\label{sec:background}

Video has become the dominant form of content on the Internet, and platforms such as
YouTube, Vimeo, and TikTok have made uploading and sharing video an everyday activity. Behind
that apparent simplicity lies a demanding engineering problem. When a user uploads a source
file, that file may use any container format, any codec, and any resolution, yet the platform
must deliver a stream that plays smoothly on every device and adapts to whatever bandwidth the
viewer happens to have at that moment.

The industry solution to this problem is HTTP Live Streaming (HLS), a protocol that splits a
video into short segments, typically between two and ten seconds long, and produces several
quality renditions of the same content. A playlist file written in the \texttt{.m3u8} format
enumerates the available renditions, and the player selects among them in real time according
to measured network throughput. This mechanism, known as Adaptive Bitrate Streaming (ABR),
is what allows playback to degrade gracefully rather than stall when a connection weakens.

Producing those renditions is computationally expensive, and this is where the architectural
difficulty arises. Transcoding is a bursty workload: it consumes a large amount of CPU while a
video is being processed and nothing at all when no one is uploading. A platform that keeps a
dedicated server cluster or a Kubernetes system running continuously simply to wait for work
therefore pays for a substantial amount of idle capacity. The opposite failure is equally
undesirable: a system provisioned only for the average case cannot absorb a sudden burst when many
users upload at the same time, and uploads either queue for a long time or are dropped.

This tension between idle cost and burst capacity motivates the project. Rather than keeping
compute permanently allocated, the system starts a container only when there is a video to process
and removes it immediately afterwards, so that cost accrues only while transcoding actually runs.

\section{Project Objectives}
\label{sec:objectives}

The project aims to build a complete video sharing platform for end users, together with the
automated transcoding subsystem that supports it, and has five objectives. The first is a web
platform on which users register an account, upload videos, watch them at several quality levels,
and share them as public, unlisted or private. The second is to automate the whole processing
pipeline, from the moment a source file lands in Amazon S3, through transcoding and storage, to
delivery through a content delivery network, until the video is ready to play in a browser. The
third is to transcode with FFmpeg inside a Docker container on AWS Batch and AWS Fargate, created
on demand and terminated automatically, so that idle resources cost nothing. The fourth is to
automate deployment and infrastructure management through a CI/CD pipeline and Infrastructure as
Code. The fifth is to evaluate the result empirically, in terms of quality of experience,
transcoding performance, scalability under load and operating cost.

The first two objectives define what the platform does for its users, and the third how the
processing is carried out; on-demand containers are the central hypothesis of the project. The
fourth makes the system reproducible and safe to change, and the fifth turns the claims of the
other four into measurements. Table~\ref{tab:objective-results} returns to each objective with its
result.

\section{Scope and Deliverables}
\label{sec:scope}

The scope of this project covers the full vertical slice of a video sharing service: the
browser-based user interface, the backend API, the transcoding engine, the cloud
infrastructure that hosts them, and the automation that deploys them.

Several areas are deliberately placed outside the scope. The project does not implement
digital rights management systems such as Widevine or FairPlay, which would be required to
prevent an authorised viewer from redistributing content they can legitimately access. It does
not implement live streaming, since the architecture is designed around video on demand. It
also does not attempt per-title bitrate ladder optimisation, using instead a fixed ladder of
six rungs from 144p to 1080p, of which each video receives those that its source resolution can
fill; the trade-off involved is discussed in Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion}.

\section{Research Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

The work combines a review of existing practice with an iterative build-and-measure cycle.

The theoretical component established how the problem is solved at industrial scale before any
code was written. Published engineering accounts from Netflix describe how a large platform
decomposes video processing into microservices and optimises a bitrate ladder per title, and the
AWS Video on Demand reference architecture describes the managed-service equivalent. The protocol
was taken from its specification, RFC 8216, and the encoding parameters from the FFmpeg HLS muxer
documentation, rather than from secondary sources. Peer-reviewed studies of serverless video
processing supplied the motivation for the architecture and the vocabulary of the evaluation, and
the multi-threaded H.264 literature supplied the bound against which the vCPU scaling results of
Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} are read.

The practical component followed an incremental development model. The backend API, the
transcoding container, the frontend and the cloud infrastructure were built and integrated in turn,
with the whole environment described as Terraform code so that every change to it is reviewable and
reproducible rather than applied by hand. An automated test suite ran on every push through a
continuous integration pipeline, which also runs dependency, container and infrastructure security
scans.

Evaluation was empirical. Every performance figure in Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} was produced by
a measurement script committed with the source code and run against the deployed system, with the
raw output stored as JSON under \path{docs/results/}; Table~\ref{tab:provenance} maps each table to
the run that produced it. Where a result could be an artefact of the measuring tool, it was
reproduced with a second, independently implemented tool before being reported.

\section{Practical Significance}
\label{sec:significance}

The immediate beneficiaries of this design are small teams and individual creators who need a
video platform but cannot justify a permanently running transcoding cluster. The cost comparison
of Section~\ref{sec:cost-analysis} estimates that processing three hundred videos a month costs
about \$3.40 on the architecture built here against \$133.35 on an always-on instance sized for the
same work, because compute exists only while a job runs. That difference makes video hosting
feasible for a service whose upload volume is intermittent, which describes most platforms before
they become large.

The design also meets a requirement that is easy to state and easy to implement incorrectly.
Marking a video private means nothing if the underlying storage stays publicly addressable, and
Chapter~\ref{chap:design} shows that restricting the API alone does not achieve it. The two-layer
scheme used here, a private bucket with authorisation enforced at the content delivery network, can
be reused by any application that serves user-owned media from object storage.

\section{Main Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

The principal contribution of this work is an end-to-end demonstration that serverless containers
can serve as the transcoding backbone of a video platform, with a measured account of what that
choice costs and what it saves. Six parts of it can be reused on their own. An upload event travels
through Amazon S3, Amazon SQS and AWS Lambda to AWS Batch with no polling service in between.
Conditional database writes make a duplicate delivery harmless, so that each job takes effect once
without relying on exactly-once delivery. Origin Access Control and CloudFront Signed Cookies keep a
private video unreachable even to a viewer who holds its manifest URL. A chunk-parallel path brings a
four-hour source to playback in 135 minutes at the production quota. A moderation pipeline and a
role-based console record every decision, so that the false positive and false negative rates of
the screening can be measured. Finally, the environment is described in thirteen Terraform modules,
and the DevSecOps pipeline blocks a merge on a fixable critical finding rather than only reporting
it.

\section{Structure of the Report}
\label{sec:structure}

The remainder of this report is organised as follows. Chapter~\ref{chap:literature} reviews
comparable platforms and the models behind the design, and justifies the technology choices.
Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements} identifies the stakeholders, states the functional and
non-functional requirements, and specifies the use cases. Chapter~\ref{chap:design} describes the
architecture, the transcoding pipeline, the database, content protection, moderation, the interface
and the API. Chapter~\ref{chap:implementation} covers the implementation of each subsystem, the
infrastructure code and the delivery pipeline, the problems met during deployment, and the automated
tests. Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation} presents the deployed system and measures playback quality,
transcoding performance, behaviour under load and operating cost, then sets the non-functional
requirements against this evidence. Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} compares the results with the
objectives and states the strengths, limitations and future work.
