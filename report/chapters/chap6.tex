\chapter{Deployment and Evaluation}
\label{chap:evaluation}

This chapter presents the system as deployed and measures it. It describes the production
environment and its interface, the method of the measurements, and then the results for playback,
transcoding, behaviour under load and cost. It ends by setting each non-functional requirement of
Chapter~\ref{chap:requirements} against the evidence.

Two choices of method affect how the numbers read. The transcoding times are measured from the moment
the upload completes, so they do not depend on the connection of the uploader. The playback runs keep
start-up delay and stalling apart, as ITU-T P.1203 does~\cite{itup1203}: a stall counts only after
the first frame has played, because waiting for a video to start and having it stop are different
experiences for a viewer.

\section{Playback}
\label{sec:qoe-evaluation}

\subsection{Time to First Frame}
\label{sec:ttff-evaluation}

Before a player can show a frame it makes five requests: the playback grant from the API, the master
playlist, a rendition playlist, the key from the API and the first segment.
Figure~\ref{fig:ttff-steps} shows how long each took. The first request after a pause took 1425~ms,
because every cache was cold: the CDN had to fetch the playlists and the segment from the bucket,
and the two calls to the API were slower too. The next 19 requests averaged 320~ms with a 95th percentile of 435~ms. Once
the files are cached at the edge, the three CDN requests together take under 50~ms, and the two
calls to the API, the grant and the key, make up more than four fifths of the time.

The rung the player settled on shows the two limits of Section~\ref{sec:abr-design} at work. In every

whose peak of 408~kbit/s fits under the link of 500~kbit/s, and still played without a stall.

Three observations follow. First, 29 to 57~seconds of every run pass before any encoding starts:
7 to 25~seconds from the upload to the end of the probe, then 19 to 35~seconds
while Batch starts Fargate tasks. This is the price of having no compute running while idle, and it
is the same for a clip and for a long video. Second, the chunked path makes time grow far more slowly
than the source: the 10-minute file is ready 25~seconds after the 2-minute one, and the 26.7-minute
file needs about twice as long as the 10-minute one for nearly three times the content. Third, the
work per second of source is nearly constant, between 4.6 and 5.8 vCPU-seconds, whichever path a
video takes. This ratio is what makes the cost of Section~\ref{sec:cost-analysis} predictable.

\subsection{A Four-Hour Video}
\label{sec:chunked-evaluation}

A four-hour 1080p video of 5.26~GiB, a synthetic moving test pattern at 30 frames per second with a
tone as its sound, was ready 38.3~minutes after its upload completed. Figure~\ref{fig:four-hour}
shows where the time went. The probe split it into 48 chunks of five minutes. The audio job encoded
the sound of the whole video in 10.8~minutes, and the chunks started only when it finished, because
each chunk copies its window of that audio. The 48 chunks then ran twelve at a time, six on Spot and
six that overflowed to On-Demand, for 26.5~minutes; a chunk took between 4 and 8~minutes. The video
reached \status{ready}
24~seconds after the last chunk, of which stitching and publishing took under 3~seconds. The run used 18.7~vCPU-hours, 4.67~vCPU-seconds per second of source, the same ratio as
the shorter files. For the 1~GB file and this run, screening was switched off so that test content
was not sent to Rekognition; its cost is added by calculation in Section~\ref{sec:cost-analysis}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/29-chart-four-hour.png}
\caption{\textit{Timeline of a four-hour video on the chunked path}}
\label{fig:four-hour}
\end{figure}

The timeline shows what limits a long video. The audio job is on the critical path for more than a
quarter of the time, and the chunks are bounded by the twelve slots that the 48~vCPUs of the two
compute environments allow. Neither is a limit of the design: the audio can be encoded in parallel
with the chunks, and the slots grow with the capacity granted. The test pattern is also easier to
encode than a recording, so a real four-hour recording would need more time per chunk.
Chapter~\ref{chap:conclusion} returns to these limits.

\section{Behaviour under Load}
\label{sec:load-testing}

\subsection{One Hundred Simultaneous Uploads}
\label{sec:burst-evaluation}

One hundred uploads of the 36-second clip were started at the same moment, twenty from each of five
accounts. Figure~\ref{fig:burst} shows every video that reached \status{ready}, sorted by how long it
took. Of the 100 uploads, 92 reached \status{ready} and none failed during processing. Eight were
refused with 429 when the upload was created, and the client gave up after three retries, so no
video existed for them. Three more received 429 on the completion call, after their file had
already reached Amazon S3; the event of S3 started their processing all the same, and the execution
history shows all three ready. The measurement tool stopped following them at the refusal, so the
figure and the times below cover the other 89. Screening was on during this run, and Rekognition
answered all 736 frames of the 92 videos, retrying the 68 calls it throttled.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/30-chart-burst.png}
\caption{\textit{One hundred simultaneous uploads: time to READY of each video, split into waiting
and processing}}
\label{fig:burst}
\end{figure}

The figure shows the queue doing its work. Each video needs an analyse task of two vCPUs and an
encode task of four, so the 48~vCPUs of the two environments process about eight videos at a time;
the rest wait in the Batch queue instead of failing. Processing itself stayed short, about
30~seconds per task, while the waiting grew steadily to ten minutes for the last video. The median
video was ready after 331~seconds and the last after 638~seconds. Both environments were used: 96
tasks ran on Spot and 82 overflowed to On-Demand.

The 11 refusals came from the API, not from the pipeline. The function log shows that all 80
instances were busy in the first minute, Lambda throttled 56 invocations, and no invocation failed.
A new instance needs about 1.1~seconds to start, and its first requests waited up to 8.8~seconds on
the free database cluster, which slows down above 100 operations per second; the 3.5~seconds that
the three retries of the client wait in total were not always enough.

\section{Cost}
\label{sec:cost-analysis}

The cost model of Section~\ref{sec:serverless-containers} assumes a month of 300 uploads of five
minutes each and 5.5~vCPU-seconds of work per second of source, the upper part of the range measured
in Table~\ref{tab:transcode-results}. That is 137.5~vCPU-hours a month. Every task uses 2~GB of memory
per vCPU, so at the Fargate On-Demand list prices of 0.05056~USD per vCPU-hour and 0.00553~USD per
GB-hour, one vCPU-hour costs 0.06162~USD. The alternative is an always-on c6i.2xlarge instance with
eight vCPUs and 16~GB, at 0.392~USD an hour for 730 hours. Screening sends one frame every five
seconds to Rekognition, 60 frames for a five-minute video, at 0.0013~USD a frame~\cite{awsrekognitionpricing}.
Table~\ref{tab:cost-comparison} gives the result.

Paying per use costs a tenth of the always-on server, and the reason is utilisation: the work of the
month fills only 2.4\% of the hours that the server is paid for. Three points qualify the comparison.
The Fargate figure uses On-Demand prices, so it is an upper bound: most tasks run on Spot, which is
cheaper. The always-on server would have one fixed capacity of eight vCPUs, so a burst like that of
Section~\ref{sec:burst-evaluation} would wait longer on it, not shorter. Storage and delivery are
left out, because they cost the same in both designs. With compute this cheap, screening becomes the
largest item; its cost grows with the number of frames sampled, which is why the sampling interval
widens for long videos (Section~\ref{sec:screening-design}).
