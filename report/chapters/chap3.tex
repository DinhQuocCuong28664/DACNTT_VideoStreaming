\chapter{Requirements Analysis}
\label{chap:requirements}

This chapter describes who uses the platform and what each group needs, states the
functional requirements (FR) and non-functional requirements (NFR) that follow from those needs, and models them as use cases (UC), each
specified step by step and checked against the routes of the API that implement it.

\section{Stakeholders and User Requirements}
\label{sec:stakeholders}

The platform serves people who watch videos, people who publish them, and staff who keep the
content within the rules. Table~\ref{tab:stakeholders} lists each group, what it needs and the
requirements of Sections~\ref{sec:functional-requirements} and \ref{sec:nonfunctional-requirements}
that answer those needs.

The anonymous visitor matters because asking for registration before any content can be seen would
deter new viewers, so the platform asks for an identity only where an action needs one: reacting,
commenting, subscribing, uploading. The viewer and the creator are the same kind of account seen
from two sides. Every registered user can watch and take part, and becomes a creator by uploading;
there is no separate creator role, as on YouTube. The creator's central need is that a large upload
neither blocks the browser nor waits on a server, and that the video becomes playable soon after the
upload ends.

The three staff roles are kept apart on purpose. An operator decides on individual videos; an
auditor reads how often the automatic screening was wrong but cannot change a decision, so the
person who reads the error rates is not the person whose decisions produce them; an administrator
manages roles and draws the random samples that measure what the screening missed. An administrator
may also act as an operator or an auditor. The development team is listed as a stakeholder because
several requirements, such as idle cost and reproducibility, serve the people who run the platform
rather than its users.

Requirement FR-03 shapes the architecture more than any other. Routing a multi-gigabyte upload
through the API would make the API pay in memory, bandwidth and execution time for every byte, and
a serverless function cannot even run long enough to receive a large file. Instead the API returns
a short-lived grant, and the browser sends the file directly to Amazon S3; the API handles only small
JSON requests whatever the size of the file. FR-04 follows from it: because the file reaches S3
without passing through the API, it is the arrival of the file in S3, not a call from the browser,
that starts the processing.

\section{Non-Functional Requirements}
\label{sec:nonfunctional-requirements}

The non-functional requirements are listed in Table~\ref{tab:nonfunctional-requirements}. Where a
quality can be measured, the requirement states a target, and Section~\ref{sec:nfr-evidence} sets
each target against the measurements of Chapter~\ref{chap:evaluation}.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Non-functional requirements of the system}
\label{tab:nonfunctional-requirements}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{p{0.08\textwidth} p{0.17\textwidth} X}
\toprule
\textbf{ID} & \textbf{ATTRIBUTE} & \textbf{REQUIREMENT} \\
\midrule
NFR-01 & Playback start & Through the CDN, the first frame appears within one second at the 95th percentile once the files are cached, and playback starts within three seconds on an unthrottled connection without stalling. \\
NFR-02 & Processing time & A video of up to ten minutes is ready within three minutes after its upload completes, and a four-hour video within one hour. \\
NFR-03 & Burst load & One hundred simultaneous uploads lose no work: every upload the API accepts reaches \status{ready}, and an upload refused under overload receives a status the client can retry. \\
NFR-04 & Idle cost & No transcoding container runs and no compute capacity is reserved or billed while nothing is being processed; functions that run on a schedule are billed per invocation. \\
NFR-05 & API latency & A warm API answers within 300~ms at the median and serves 80 concurrent requests without errors. \\
NFR-06 & Content security & Segments, playlists and keys of a video are unreachable without a grant for that video, even to someone who knows their addresses. \\
NFR-07 & API security & Sign-in and other sensitive routes are rate-limited, only the web origins of the platform may call the API from a browser, and the function behind the API answers only requests that came through its gateway. \\
NFR-08 & Reliability & A failed processing step is retried when a retry can succeed; otherwise the video is marked failed with a reason and an alert is sent. No video stays in processing for more than a day. \\
NFR-09 & Reproducibility & Every environment is created from the same infrastructure code, with no manual changes. \\
NFR-10 & Maintainability & A change reaches the main branch only after linting, type checks, tests and security scans pass. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

NFR-06 goes beyond ordinary access control. Restricting what the API returns is straightforward,
but the API does not deliver the video: the content delivery network delivers it as hundreds of
small files, and a single leaked address would otherwise be enough to download them. The design of
Chapter~\ref{chap:design} therefore protects the files themselves, not only the API. NFR-03 is
phrased around accepted work because a burst can exceed what a free database tier and a fixed
concurrency limit absorb at once; what must never happen is that an accepted upload silently
disappears.

\midrule
Actor & Processing system \\
Requirements & FR-04, FR-18, NFR-02, NFR-03, NFR-08 \\
Precondition & A source file has arrived in the raw bucket. \\
Main flow &
  1. Amazon S3 emits an event, which Amazon SQS buffers. \newline
  2. A function reads the metadata of the object and starts a workflow execution named after the video and its upload version. \newline
  3. The workflow marks the video as processing, creates its encryption key and probes the source. \newline
  4. In parallel, one job screens sampled frames and makes the thumbnail and seek-preview images, while the encoding runs as one job for a video of up to five minutes, or as an audio job and parallel chunk jobs followed by a stitching step for a longer one. \newline

  4b. A job fails for a reason that another attempt cannot fix, such as an unreadable source: the video becomes \status{error} with the reason, the owner is told once, and an alert is sent. \newline
  4c. The screening cannot run: the video is held for review. \newline
  5a. The screening removes the video or holds it: it is not listed, and its owner may appeal. \\
